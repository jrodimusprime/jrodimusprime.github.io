Stop Remodelling the Companies You Buy

When a company buys another, the data team spends a year or more forcing the new business's data into the enterprise data model, so the reports line up. AI can read both companies' data as they are, which makes most of that remodelling a cost with no return: a few million dollars and a year of delay on every deal.

Jared Ryan · 4 November 2026

Executive statement

Every acquisition triggers the same ritual: remap the new company's customers, products and transactions into the parent's enterprise model, rebuild the pipelines, reconcile, and only then report on the combined business. AI makes that remodel unnecessary. Keep their data as it is, link the shared keys, write their definitions down, and ask questions across both companies within weeks.

The bigger prize is time. Advisers commonly attribute 30–50% of deal synergies to IT and data integration, and those synergies wait until the combined numbers exist. The figures are illustrative arithmetic on a mid-sized deal; scale them to your team and deal size.

Conform the books. Leave the rest of the acquired company's data as it is.

Why we've always remodelled

The reasons were real. An enterprise data model (Bill Inmon's "single version of the truth", or Ralph Kimball's conformed dimensions) means every report uses one definition of customer, product, region and revenue. When you buy a company, its data arrives in a different shape: its customer table, product hierarchy, chart of accounts and its own idea of what an "active" customer is. A dashboard can't read two shapes at once, and an analyst can't be trusted to reconcile them by hand each time. So the new company gets remodelled to fit:

  • map every field into the parent model;
  • rebuild the pipelines and backfill the history;
  • reconcile until the numbers tie out.

Planning guides for this work commonly quote 12 to 24 months. Gartner has estimated that IT accounts for about a quarter of total integration effort. Meanwhile, by Clayton Christensen's widely quoted estimate in Harvard Business Review, 70–90% of acquisitions fail to deliver what was promised, and slow integration is one of the usual culprits.

Why it's wasteful now

The remodel exists because the reader needed one shape. The reader has changed. AI can read the acquired company's data in its own shape, work out how it relates to yours, and answer a question across both, with no shared model built in advance. The research has moved fast:

  • Matching records: Narayan, Chami, Orr and Ré (VLDB 2022) found that GPT-3 with only a few examples beat specialised, fully trained systems at the core integration tasks: deciding whether two records are the same thing, spotting errors and filling gaps.
  • Unseen data: Peeters, Steiner and Bizer (EDBT 2025) found GPT-4 matched customer and product records as well as models trained on large labelled datasets, without that training. It also held up far better on records it had never seen, which is exactly what an acquisition hands you.
  • Matching schemas: deciding that their cust_acct is your customer_id is now a working research area. Magneto (2024–25) pairs small and large language models to do it accurately at scale.
  • Context: written definitions make AI far more accurate on enterprise data. In Sequeda, Allemang and Jacob's benchmark, accuracy went from 16.7% to 54.2% (see "AI Is the Knowledge Layer Now").
You bought the company for what it knows. Then you spent a year deleting it to fit your schema.

There's a hidden cost too. Forcing an acquisition into the parent's model throws away what made it different: its own product groupings, its way of measuring margin, the fields its people actually used. Those are often why you bought it. The data-mesh movement (Zhamak Dehghani, 2019) argued for leaving data with the domain that understands it. AI is what makes that workable: the meaning stays with the data, and the AI translates when someone asks a question across both companies.

What you still do, in weeks

Keep doingWhy it stays
Land their raw data, unchangedThe record of what happened. AI can work out meaning on demand, but it can't recover data you didn't keep.
Link the shared keysWhich of their customers, products, suppliers and staff are also yours. That's a fact, not an inference, and it stops double counting. AI suggests the matches; people approve them.
Write down their definitionsTheir "active customer", their revenue rules, their product groupings, as context the AI reads. This keeps what the business knows instead of overwriting it.
Map the general ledgerConsolidated financial statements must be exact and repeatable, and that's a legal requirement, not a modelling preference. Map their chart of accounts to the group's; it's a few hundred lines, not a warehouse.
Apply their privacy termsTheir customers agreed to their privacy policy, not yours. Access rules come over before any AI can read across both companies.

What you stop doing: rebuilding their transaction history in your model, conforming every dimension, and the months of reconciliation that follow.

Where it breaks

  • Bad matches. A wrong customer match double-counts revenue or merges two people. Have people review AI matches before they count, especially for the largest accounts.
  • Silent definition clashes. If one company books revenue gross and the other net, an AI combining them without written definitions will add apples to oranges. Definitions come before questions.
  • The books. Group financial statements, regulatory returns and covenants stay exact and reconciled. Don't ask an AI to work them out fresh each time.
  • System retirement is a different decision. Switching off their ERP to save licences may still be worth it. Even then, move their data across as raw data plus written context, and don't rebuild it in your model.

For the next deal

  • 1. Write "no remodel" into the integration plan. The data workstream scope covers raw landing, key linking, definitions, the GL mapping and access rules. Nothing else is conformed unless a regulator or the board needs it.
  • 2. Measure the first combined answer. Target weeks, not quarters.
  • 3. Keep their meaning. Their definitions sit beside yours, versioned like code, so nothing they knew is lost.
  • 4. Store only what's reused. If a combined question is asked every week, cache the answer with its definition (see the knowledge layer piece).

The enterprise data model was how a company made its data readable to people and dashboards. AI reads the data as it is. The next acquisition doesn't need a year of remodelling, only its keys linked, its definitions written down, and its books mapped.

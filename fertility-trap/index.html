Don't Pay for Babies. Fix the Trap.

Japan treated falling births as a dip it could buy its way out of. Thirty-five years and trillions of yen later, it has the fewest births on record. Australia is walking the same path, and the exit isn't a bigger cheque.

Jared Ryan · 4 November 2026

In 1990, Japan got its "1.57 shock". The fertility rate had fallen to 1.57 children per woman, and Tokyo responded the way governments respond to a downturn: with money and plans. There was the Angel Plan in 1994, the New Angel Plan in 1999, a childbirth grant now worth ¥500,000, and in 2023 a ¥3.6 trillion-a-year package billed as action "of a different dimension". In 2024 Japan's fertility rate hit 1.15, with about 686,000 births, the lowest since records began.

Australia's rate is 1.48 (ABS, 2024), a record low. We're not Japan yet. But we have the same structural faults, and we've already tried Japan's medicine once.

Why the cheques failed

Australia's Baby Bonus (2004–2014) coincided with a rise in fertility, which peaked at 2.02 in 2008. But researchers who studied it (Drago and colleagues, 2011) found only a small effect, and much of it was timing: women had the babies they'd planned, just sooner. The rate has fallen almost every year since.

Japan shows why. Fertility didn't fall because babies were too expensive at birth. It fell because a baby forced a choice:

  • Women had to choose between a career and a family. For decades, roughly six in ten working Japanese women left their job around their first birth. In 2021, husbands of women with a child under six did 1 hour 54 minutes of housework and childcare a day. Their wives did 7 hours 28 minutes.
  • Men couldn't afford to start. Japan split its job market in two: permanent jobs for insiders, and insecure "non-regular" work for everyone else. Men in non-regular jobs are far less likely to marry. In Japan, almost no one has children outside marriage, so fewer marriages means fewer births.
  • Leave existed only on paper. UNICEF ranked Japan's paid leave for fathers the most generous in the rich world. Almost no one used it: uptake was about 3% a decade ago, because taking it signalled you weren't serious about your job.
A one-off payment can't fix twenty years of a lost career. People can do the sums.

What the research says now

The old rule was that richer, more educated women have fewer children. In the rich world that rule has flipped. Since about 2000, the countries with more women in work have had higher fertility, not lower (Doepke, Hannusch, Kindermann & Tertilt, NBER 2022). What predicts births now is whether a family can manage two jobs and children.

Nobel laureate Claudia Goldin puts the lowest fertility in countries where the economy modernised fast but expectations of men at home and at work didn't keep up: Japan, South Korea, Italy, Spain ("Babies and the Macroeconomy", 2023). The cost women pay is measurable: in Australia, mothers' earnings fall by about 55% in the five years after a first child, while fathers' barely move (Treasury, Bahar et al., 2022).

Australia's version of the trap

  • Housing. About 60% of Australians aged 25–34 owned a home in 1981. Today it's under half. Australian research finds rising house prices increase births among owners and reduce them among renters (Atalay, Li & Whelan, "Housing wealth and fertility: Australian evidence"). A spare room is a precondition, and so is not having to move every year.
  • Leave paid at the minimum wage. Paid parental leave goes to 26 weeks in July 2026, which is real progress. But it pays the national minimum wage, so in most couples, especially where the father earns more, he can't afford to take it. That's Japan's paper-leave problem with an Australian accent.
  • Insecure early careers. Casual and gig work, HECS debts and rent arrive in exactly the years people would otherwise partner up. That hits young men the way Japan's two-tier job market did.

Five fixes that aren't immigration

  • 1. Treat childcare as infrastructure. The 3 Day Guarantee (from January 2026) is a start. The goal is France's model: cheap, universal care from infancy and school from age three, plus before- and after-school care that matches real working hours.
  • 2. Reserve leave for dads, and pay it at their wage. When Quebec reserved five well-paid weeks for fathers in 2006, fathers' uptake jumped by over 50 percentage points. The fathers who took leave were still doing more at home years later (Patnaik, Journal of Labor Economics, 2019). Japan's lesson is that the leave has to be the default, not a favour.
  • 3. Stop rewarding "greedy jobs". Goldin's term for jobs that pay a premium for unlimited hours. Make strong rights to flexible and part-time work the norm, with no career penalty, in the public service first so the private sector follows.
  • 4. Make housing policy fertility policy. Build more homes near jobs, and give renters secure long-term leases. It will do more for births than any bonus.
  • 5. Give young adults a secure start. A path from casual to permanent work, and HECS repayments that don't bite hardest at 28. People partner up when the future looks solid.

The honest part

No rich country has got back to 2.1. Even Finland, with world-class family policy, has slid to about 1.25, so family policy alone isn't enough. South Korea has spent more than 380 trillion won since 2006 and sits at 0.75. The realistic goal isn't a baby boom. It's closing the gap between the children Australians say they want and the number they actually have, and that gap is mostly about housing, careers and who does the work at home. Japan spent 35 years paying for the symptom. Australia can fix the cause.

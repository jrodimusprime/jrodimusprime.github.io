"Get on the Ladder," They Said

A property bubble only keeps rising while new buyers keep arriving at higher prices. For forty years, the people already on the ladder have told the young to climb on. They didn't need a conspiracy to do it. Their wealth depended on it.

Jared Ryan · 4 November 2026

Every Australian has heard it. Rent money is dead money. You can't lose with bricks and mortar. Just get a foot in the door. Prices only go one way. It is usually said kindly, by parents, bank managers, property shows and politicians. It is also the one piece of advice that a property bubble needs above all others, because a bubble isn't held up by the people who already own. It's held up by the people who haven't bought yet.

How a bubble actually works

A house price is set by the last buyer. If you bought in 1995, your "wealth" today exists only because someone younger has just paid a record price for the house next door. Economist Hyman Minsky described how booms move from borrowing you can repay from income to borrowing that only works if prices keep rising. At that point the market needs a steady stream of new entrants, each paying more and borrowing more than the last.

Research shows how that stream is recruited:

  • Through friends and family. People whose friends saw big house-price gains are more likely to buy, buy bigger and pay more (Bailey, Cao, Kuchler & Stroebel, Journal of Political Economy, 2018).
  • Like a contagion. Optimism about house prices spreads from person to person, and booms and busts follow when it does (Burnside, Eichenbaum & Rebelo, JPE, 2016).
  • Through stories. Robert Shiller showed that "prices always go up" narratives feed on recent price rises, which pull in more buyers, which make the story true for a while.
The advice to "get on the ladder" is the bubble's recruitment drive, run by everyone it has already paid.

Who benefits when you climb on

About two-thirds of Australian households own their home. For them, every new buyer who pays a little more is a quiet pay rise. The people giving the advice all gain from it being taken:

  • Owners, whose paper wealth is confirmed by every higher sale.
  • Banks, whose balance sheets are mostly mortgages, and which earn more on bigger loans.
  • Governments, which collect stamp duty on every sale and win votes from rising house values.
  • Agents and property media, paid by volume and by price.

None of this requires anyone to be cynical. A parent who says "buy now, whatever it takes" usually means it, and it worked for them. But it worked for them because the next generation kept following the same advice at higher and higher prices.

Japan ran out of new buyers

Japan's 1980s land bubble ran the same way. As prices outran incomes, banks stretched to keep buyers coming: loans lasting 100 years, and "two-generation" mortgages that a buyer's children would finish paying. In March 1990 the finance ministry capped bank lending to property. The flow of new buyers stopped and prices fell for years. Land in the big cities lost most of its value. Those hurt most were the last ones in, young families who bought at the top, encouraged by everyone who had bought before them.

Australia keeps finding new ways to bring buyers in

Each time prices outrun what young buyers can pay, a new way is found to help them pay it:

  • Grants: the First Home Owner Grant (2000) and its 2008 boost. The Productivity Commission's 2004 inquiry found that handing buyers cash mostly pushes up prices rather than helping buyers.
  • Smaller deposits: since October 2025, the Home Guarantee Scheme lets any first buyer in with a 5% deposit, with no income cap and a higher price cap.
  • Super: the First Home Super Saver Scheme lets buyers draw on retirement savings for a deposit.
  • Family money: the "Bank of Mum and Dad" is estimated to rank among the country's ten biggest lenders.

Each one is sold as help for the young. Each one adds buying power to a market where the number of homes barely changes, so it shows up in the price. Australian homes now cost roughly twice as much relative to incomes as they did in the early 1990s. The young are not being helped onto the ladder. They're being recruited to hold it up.

How to stop the recruiting

  • Stop subsidising demand. No more grants, deposit schemes or super drawdowns that add buying power without adding homes.
  • Build. More homes is the only kind of help that doesn't get absorbed into the price.
  • Tax homes as homes, not lottery tickets. Wind back the tax breaks that reward buying for the capital gain (see "Rich by Default").
  • Tell the truth about price rises. A rising house price isn't wealth for the country. It's a transfer from the next buyer to the last one.

The older generation didn't plan a pyramid. But when a system only works if the young keep paying more, and the people it rewards keep telling the young to pay, the outcome is the same. The kindest advice for a 25-year-old is the opposite of what they've been told: don't let anyone, including your parents, tell you a price can only go up.

Stop Building the Enterprise Data Model

For forty years, companies have paid teams of modellers to draw one great model of the whole business and force every system's data into it. Most of those programmes ran for years, cost millions and were out of date on delivery. AI makes the big model unnecessary. What it can't do without is the part the model was always meant to capture: what the data means.

Jared Ryan · 4 November 2026

Executive statement

The enterprise data model was a way to make data readable to people and dashboards: every customer, product and transaction forced into one approved structure. AI reads data in whatever structure it arrives in. Stop funding the big model. Fund a living glossary of what things mean, plus the keys that link them, and model new concepts in days as questions need them.

The larger, hidden saving is the change tax: every new source system, product or acquisition currently waits for a model change request before anyone can use the data. The figures are illustrative arithmetic; scale them to your organisation.

Model the meaning, not the tables.

What the enterprise model was for

The idea is older than the data warehouse. John Zachman's 1987 framework, James Martin's information engineering and Bill Inmon's normalised enterprise warehouse all aimed at one integrated model of the business, drawn by experts and imposed on every system. Vendors sold ready-made versions: IBM's banking and insurance models, Teradata's financial services model (FSLDM), the insurance industry's OMG property-and-casualty model. Data Vault offered a way to bolt every new source onto one structure.

The reasons were sound. If every system calls a customer something different, nobody can count customers. People and reporting tools need one structure they can understand and trust. Before data reaches anyone, someone has to decide what a policy, an account or a household is.

The track record was poor:

  • In 2005 Gartner forecast that more than half of data warehouse projects would have limited acceptance or fail outright.
  • A 2012 Dresner survey found only 41% were considered successful.
  • Even IBM's own commissioned study of its industry models made the cost visible: buying a ready-made model was sold on cutting modelling time by 30–40%, because modelling is where the time goes.
  • The model itself was rarely finished. The business kept changing faster than the model could be redrawn.

Why it's mostly pointless now

The big model existed so that readers could understand the data. The new reader doesn't need it. An AI agent can read a source system's own schema, work out that cust_acct in one system and client_no in another are the same thing, and join them when a question needs it. Making all the data fit one approved shape in advance no longer earns its cost.

But the research makes the argument more interesting than "modelling is dead":

  • Meaning made the difference. In Sequeda, Allemang and Jacob's 2023 benchmark, GPT-4 answered enterprise questions over an insurance database with 16.7% accuracy. Given a knowledge graph that described what the tables and fields mean, built on the OMG insurance industry model, accuracy rose to 54.2%. The payoff didn't come from physically conforming the data to the industry model. It came from writing down the model's meaning where the AI could read it.
  • AI can't design the big model for you. Since Fill, Fettke and Köpke's first experiments with ChatGPT in 2023, researchers have tested AI at drawing data models from requirements. A 2026 study found reliability falls as requirements get more complex, with more inconsistencies and missed elements. That's not a problem if you stop trying to build one model of everything. It's a problem only if you ask AI to do the old job faster.
The model's value was always the meaning. The structure was just the only way to deliver that meaning to readers who couldn't reason.

Where the old model still earns its keep

  • Systems that write data. An order system or core banking platform still needs a careful, normalised design. Codd's rules exist to stop data contradicting itself as it's written, and AI doesn't change that. This article is about the analytics and integration model, not application databases.
  • Regulated reporting. Financial statements, prudential returns and Basel risk reporting need fixed, repeatable structures. In the Basel Committee's 2023 review, only 2 of 31 global systemically important banks fully met its risk-data principles. Keep those models, and keep them reconciled.
  • Without a glossary, it's worse than before. If you drop the model and don't write down the meaning, every AI agent invents its own definitions, and you get chaos faster than the old model ever produced it. The 16.7% figure is what that looks like.
  • Performance. Some questions are asked thousands of times a day. Store those answers; that's caching, not enterprise modelling.

What to do instead

Instead ofDo this
A multi-year enterprise model buildA glossary of the 50–200 concepts people actually ask about, written in weeks and growing as questions arrive
Model change requests before data can be usedLand the data as it is; add its definitions when someone first needs them
Implementing an industry modelBorrow its vocabulary for your glossary, without restructuring your data to match it
A modelling team that draws structuresA small team that owns meaning, keys and test questions
Measuring model coverageMeasuring questions answered correctly against known answers

Enterprise data modelling was never really about the diagram. It was about getting a whole company to agree on what its words mean. That agreement is still the hard part, and still worth paying for.

Japan Waited for the Inheritance. Don't.

Japan had the biggest pile of family wealth in the world and a plan to let it trickle down. Five structural mistakes meant it reached the young thirty years too late. Australia is making the same five.

Jared Ryan · 4 November 2026

Japanese households hold about ¥2,200 trillion in financial assets, and over 60% of it belongs to people aged 60 and over. For a generation, Tokyo treated that as the solution: the money would pass to the children eventually. It did, but those children were in their 60s by then. Japan has a word for it: rōrō sōzoku, "old-to-old inheritance".

Japan's five mistakes

  • 1. It protected insiders. After the 1991 crash, companies kept lifetime jobs and seniority pay for older staff, and froze hiring of graduates. The share of workers in insecure "non-regular" jobs rose from 20% to about 37%. Graduates from 1993 to 2005, the "Employment Ice Age", never recovered.
  • 2. It let the money sit. Older households kept their savings in bank deposits. With interest rates near zero, those savings earned nothing, and older people saved even harder against long lives and care costs.
  • 3. It let housing rot. Japanese houses are written off in about 22 years, and land prices fell for a quarter-century. Many heirs inherited a liability, not an asset.
  • 4. It fixed tax, not timing. Tax-free gifts for grandchildren's education, weddings and homes helped only families with millions to spare. The 2015 inheritance tax rise taxed more estates (up from 4.4% to about 8%) but didn't make the money arrive any earlier.
  • 5. It governed for the old. Voters in their 60s turn out at twice the rate of voters in their 20s (about 71% against 36% in 2021), so pensions came first and young families last.

The bill was paid in births. The Ice Age cohort were the children of Japan's baby boom, and demographers expected them to deliver a third boom around 2000. In insecure jobs, with no help until their 60s, they didn't.

An inheritance at 60 can make you a grandparent. It can't make you a parent.

Australia is lining up the same five

Australia's own "great wealth transfer" is put at $3.5 trillion by 2050 (Productivity Commission). The most common age to inherit from a parent is already 55 to 59. The pattern is familiar:

  • Insiders protected: tax breaks on property (negative gearing, the capital gains discount) favour people who already own.
  • Money stuck: stamp duty punishes older owners for moving, so big houses stay with small households.
  • Help blocked: a pensioner who gives a child more than $10,000 a year ($30,000 over five years) is treated by Centrelink as still holding the extra, and can lose pension for it.
  • Tax tweaks over timing: super is meant to fund retirement, but it is increasingly passed on untouched as an inheritance.
  • Old votes first: housing and retirement policy is written for the people who turn out.

What Australia should do instead

  • Move the transfer earlier. Raise the pension gifting limits so parents can help with a deposit or childcare at 30, not leave a house at 85.
  • Swap stamp duty for land tax. The ACT has been doing it since 2012. It frees older owners to downsize and puts homes back on the market.
  • Stop rewarding the people who already own. Wind back property tax breaks that favour existing owners, and build enough homes near jobs that a 30-year-old can buy one without an inheritance.
  • Make the first job secure. Japan shows that a generation that starts in insecure work never catches up. Create real paths from casual work into permanent jobs, and keep cutting student debt; the 2025 cut was a start.
  • Write the budget for 30-year-olds. Test every major tax and spending decision on what it does for people aged 25 to 40, the years when families get started.

Japan's lesson is blunt: a country can be rich and still run out of children, if the wealth sits with people too old to use it for a family. Australia can't wait for the inheritance. It has to move the help to the years that matter. For the birth-rate side of the same problem, see "Don't Pay for Babies. Fix the Trap."

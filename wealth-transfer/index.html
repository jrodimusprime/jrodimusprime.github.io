The Great Wealth Transfer Has a Hole in It

Australians have been promised a $3.5 trillion inheritance boom. Add 5% inflation, tax, slower immigration and a strained aged care system, and a $1.7 million estate can shrink to less than $50,000 in today's money.

Jared Ryan · 21 October 2026

The "great wealth transfer" is sold as a lifeline for younger Australians locked out of housing. Our modelling of one typical retired couple tells a harsher story. In a Japan-style economy with a care worker shortage, almost everything they own is spent before it can be passed on, even after a nursing home stay of just three years.

Even in milder scenarios, the inheritance arrives late, arrives smaller, and mostly lands with people who are already well off.

A big number with a short memory

The headline figure comes from a 2021 Productivity Commission paper estimating that about $3.5 trillion will change hands in Australia by 2050. That works out to roughly $175 billion a year. Stockbroker JBWere has since revised it to about $5.4 trillion. Nobody became more generous; house prices simply went up.

The number is real, but it is a gross total spread over decades. It counts houses and super changing names on a title, not money landing in young people's bank accounts. Much of the excitement comes from wealth managers and advisers whose business is capturing those flows.

The details deflate it fast:

  • It arrives late. The most common age to inherit from a parent is 55 to 59, long after the mortgage and childcare years.
  • It's smaller than it sounds. The average inheritance was $125,000, but the median was $45,000.
  • It flows uphill. The wealthiest fifth of Australians receive 38% of inheritance money; the poorest fifth get 8%.
  • Many get nothing. A third of Australians surveyed by Finder said they don't expect any inheritance.

We ran the numbers

We modelled a couple aged 70 with a $1.2 million house and $500,000 in super, a starting wealth of $1.7 million. One partner dies at 83; the survivor moves into a nursing home at 85 and the house is sold to pay for it. We tested two economies, three care-cost regimes and two lengths of stay.

Every scenario uses a pessimistic backdrop: 5% inflation for two decades, savings rates below inflation, income tax, and slower immigration that weakens house prices and deepens the shortage of care workers.

Every dollar figure here is in today's money

At 5% inflation, prices double about every 14 years. An inheritance that arrives in 20 years will be a bigger number on the cheque than it is worth to you now: $100,000 paid in 2046 buys what about $37,700 buys today. So every amount in this article has been converted back to 2026 dollars, and where it matters we also show what the cheque itself would say.

Two families, two endings

These two households are illustrative, built from the model rather than real people. Each has two children, and both retire at 70 into the same pessimistic backdrop.

Sue and Dave, outer suburbs. They own a $750,000 house and have $200,000 in super, living on a modest budget topped up by the Age Pension. Markets behave normally, but a care shortage scraps the fee caps. Dave dies at 83; Sue moves into a nursing home at 85 and stays five years. Her care bill peaks at about $141,000 a year in today's money, and she pays $68,000 in tax across retirement. When she dies at 90, her two children, now around 60, split about $207,000 in today's money. That's roughly $104,000 each, from a starting wealth of $950,000. The cheque in 2046 would actually read about $549,000, or $275,000 each. But prices will be 2.7 times higher by then, so it buys only what $207,000 buys today.

Anne and Peter, inner Melbourne. They start with a $1.2 million house and $500,000 in super, and live comfortably. Then the economy turns Japan-style. Their super runs dry at 82, forcing $121,000 in spending cuts before either of them needs care. Peter dies at 83. When Anne enters care at 85, the house sells for $682,000 in today's money, barely half its value when they retired. After a seven-year stay, with fees peaking near $151,000 a year, the government has picked up $298,000 of her costs. Her children, now in their early 60s, share about $54,000 in today's money: $27,000 each from a $1.7 million start. Their 2048 cheque would read about $158,000, or $79,000 each, which sounds a lot better than it is.

What eats the inheritance

Care policy matters more than the sharemarket. A Japan-style crash with today's aged care rules and a seven-year stay still leaves about $308,000. Normal markets with fully private care costs leave about $57,000. If worker shortages push governments to scrap fee caps, that does more damage to inheritances than any market slump.

The rules are already tightening. Under the new Aged Care Act, from 1 November 2025 about half of new residents pay more. Non-clinical care can cost up to $101.16 a day, providers keep 2% of the room deposit each year for five years, and means-tested fees are capped at $130,000 over a lifetime. That cap is what stands between a family and the worst outcomes in our model.

Inflation quietly eats the room deposit. The deposit is refunded as a fixed dollar amount. With 5% inflation and the annual retention, a $500,000 deposit comes back worth about $406,000 in today's money after three years, and $320,000 after seven. In most of the bad scenarios, it is the biggest thing left.

The super is gone first. In every Japan-style run, the couple's $500,000 in super is exhausted before care begins. They cut their spending by $105,000 to $121,000 in total through their late 70s and early 80s. The squeeze hits their own living standard years before it hits their children's inheritance.

The tax office takes a slice of a shrinking pie. Interest on house-sale proceeds is taxed in full, even when inflation means the saver is losing money in real terms. Tax brackets and the seniors' offset aren't automatically indexed, so rising pension payments drift into tax. Across the scenarios, the household pays $35,000 to $90,000 in tax in today's dollars. Any super left over is taxed at 17% on its taxable portion when it passes to adult children.

Lower immigration bites twice. Migrants make up 59% of workers in residential care, so a smaller intake means fewer carers and faster-rising care costs. It also softens demand for housing, shaving value off the one asset most families are counting on.

Fewer children cuts both ways. Smaller families mean bigger shares for each child. But fewer adult children also means fewer unpaid carers, pushing more elderly people into paid care that drains the estate.

The bottom line

The great wealth transfer isn't a lie, but under a pessimistic outlook it is badly oversold. In a bad economy, a family like this one can pass on close to nothing, even after a short care stay. In the more typical case, a stay of a few years with today's caps intact, heirs receive about a sixth to a quarter of starting wealth in a Japan-style economy, and roughly a third to two-thirds in normal markets.

Either way, the money arrives in heirs' late 50s, after care providers, inflation and the couple's own living costs have taken their share. It won't fix housing for the young. If anything, it will widen the gap between those who inherit and those who don't.

How we modelled it

A year-by-year cash-flow model in today's dollars, with 5% inflation throughout. The couple is 70, with a $1.2 million house owned outright and $500,000 in super, spending around a "comfortable" retirement budget. They draw a part Age Pension under the assets test, indexed to prices only. They take the legal minimum super withdrawals and pay for home help from age 80.

One partner dies at 83. The survivor enters residential care at 85, the house is sold with 3% selling costs, and a $500,000 room deposit is paid. Further fees come from savings, then the deposit, down to a protected floor of about $63,000.

All results are reported in 2026 dollars: each future amount is divided by 1.05 for every year until it is paid. To get the amount heirs would actually receive in that year, multiply back, by about 2.65 for 2046 and 2.93 for 2048.

Scenario settings:

  • Japan-style markets: houses fall 3.5% a year in real terms, shares 5% and cash 2%. Normal markets: houses rise 0.5%, shares 3%, cash falls 1%.
  • Lower immigration: house prices run 0.5 points a year weaker than otherwise, and care costs in the shortage scenarios rise 4% a year faster than inflation.
  • Tax: 2026-27 income tax rates apply to the Age Pension and interest, with brackets and the seniors' offset frozen. Super pensions are tax-free over 60. Leftover super passing to adult children is taxed at 17% on an assumed 90% taxable portion.

The Sue and Dave case uses a modest budget of $48,000 a year as a couple and $34,000 single, and a $400,000 room deposit. Children's ages assume they were born when their parents were 30.

The model is simplified. It ignores tax, couples entering care together, and some details of the pension and aged care means tests. Pension rates and thresholds are approximate. Treat the results as indicative, not as financial advice.

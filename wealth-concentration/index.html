Wealth · Japan & Australia

Rich by Default

Nobody voted for wealth to pile up at the top. Japan and Australia built machines that did it automatically: cheap money, tax breaks for people who already own, and index money that keeps buying whatever is already biggest. Then they called rising prices success.

Jared Ryan · 4 November 2026

An asset bubble doesn't create wealth. It moves it from the people who will buy later to the people who own now. Run that for thirty years and you get Japan's over-60s holding most of the country's financial assets, and an Australia where a 30-year-old needs an inheritance to buy a house. Neither country planned it. Both had a system that ran on autopilot.

The machine has three parts

  • Cheap money. Low interest rates make every asset worth more on paper, so whoever already owns assets gains, and wages don't.
  • Tax that rewards owning. If gains on assets are taxed more lightly than wages, the system quietly pays people for having been early.
  • Automatic buying. Compulsory savings and index funds pour money in every payday, whatever the price, into whatever is already largest.

Japan: the land myth, then the central bank as index fund

After the 1985 Plaza Accord, the Bank of Japan cut rates to 2.5% and kept them there until 1989. Banks lent against land on the belief known as tochi shinwa, the "land myth": land prices never fall. At the peak, the grounds of Tokyo's Imperial Palace were said to be worth more than all the real estate in California. The Nikkei peaked at 38,915 in December 1989 and didn't get back there until February 2024, 34 years later. Commercial land in the big cities lost more than 80% of its value.

The clean-up protected the owners. Banks hid bad loans for a decade, and zombie firms were kept alive while graduate hiring froze. Then the central bank became the market's biggest passive buyer. From 2010 the Bank of Japan bought stock-market index funds (ETFs). By 2020 it was the largest single owner of Japanese shares, at around 7% of the market. It stopped buying in 2024. Index buying of that size props up the price of everything in the index, and the people who gain are the ones who already hold shares: overwhelmingly older and wealthier households.

Australia: the house as a tax shelter

Australia restored negative gearing in 1987. In 1999 it cut the tax on capital gains in half. Together they let investors deduct the losses on a property against their wages, then pay tax on only half the gain when they sell. Investor lending boomed, and home prices have since roughly doubled relative to incomes.

Supply was rationed at the same time. Reserve Bank researchers estimated that zoning rules added 73% to the price of a detached house in Sydney (Kendall & Tulip, 2018). In the pandemic the cash rate went to 0.1% and the RBA lent banks $188 billion at almost no cost. National home values rose 22% in 2021 alone. Australia's homes are now worth about $11 trillion, several times the value of the whole sharemarket.

Every rate cut and every tax break was sold as help for buyers. Each one was mostly paid to sellers.

Australia: super as the automatic buyer

Compulsory super, introduced in 1992, now holds over $4 trillion, and its tax breaks flow mostly to high earners (Retirement Income Review, 2020). Since 2021, funds have been tested each year against benchmark indexes, so straying from the index risks failing the test. Those benchmarks are dominated by a handful of banks and miners.

Research on inelastic markets shows why this matters: each extra dollar that flows into shares raises total market value by about $5 (Gabaix & Koijen, 2021). Money that must be invested whatever the price doesn't just buy assets; it inflates them, and the gains go to whoever already holds them. Our own lab saw the same thing up close: shares pushed into an index jumped on the forced buying and gave the gain back over the following weeks (more in "The Index Fund That Ate the World").

Who ends up holding it

In Japan, over 60% of household financial assets belong to people aged 60 and over. In Australia, the wealthiest 20% of households hold about 60% of all household wealth (ABS), and the bottom 20% hold about 1%. These aren't the result of a conspiracy. They're what you get when every lever points the same way for thirty years.

What Australia should change

  • Tax gains like wages. Wind back the 50% capital gains discount and negative gearing for new purchases. Stop paying people for timing.
  • Build. Zoning reform is the single biggest price lever the RBA has measured. Supply, not grants to buyers.
  • Count asset prices as a cost. Rate cuts that inflate housing are a transfer to owners. APRA's 2017 cap on interest-only lending showed that lending rules can cool investor demand without raising rates for everyone.
  • Cap the super tax breaks at the top. Compulsory savings should build retirement incomes, not untaxed estates.
  • Don't make index-hugging compulsory. Let the super performance test reward results, not closeness to a benchmark.

Japan shows where this ends: a rich country where the wealth sits with people too old to use it, and a young generation that stopped having children. That story is told in "Japan Waited for the Inheritance. Don't." and "Don't Pay for Babies. Fix the Trap."

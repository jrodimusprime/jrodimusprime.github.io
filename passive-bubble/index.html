The Index Fund That Ate the World

Index funds promise about 7% a year above inflation, forever. Compound that for a century and paying their owners would take more than four times the entire world's output, every year. Passive investing is making a promise the planet can't keep.

Jared Ryan · 21 October 2026

The pitch is simple and, for one person, true. Put money in a fund that owns the whole market, don't touch it, and history says it grows about 10% a year, or 7% after inflation. Australians do it automatically through super. Americans do it through their retirement plans. Index funds and ETFs now hold more of America's fund money than active managers do.

But a promise that works for one saver is not a plan that works for everyone at once. So we asked: what happens if the whole system keeps compounding at the historical average for the next hundred years?

The world's shares are already worth about 1.35 years of everything humanity produces, a record. In the United States the ratio is above 2. ETFs, almost all of them index trackers, have grown about 21% a year for a decade. Since the end of 2023, index funds have held more of America's fund money than active managers, and 55% by the end of 2025.

Three ways to project a century

We projected three paths, all in today's dollars, against a world economy that keeps growing at 3% a year after inflation, close to its recent pace.

  • Index funds keep growing like the last ten years. At about 18% a year after inflation, they would own every listed share on the planet by around 2043 to 2045, and be worth more than all the wealth on Earth by 2048. By 2126 they would be worth 149,000 years of world output. That path ends within twenty years, one way or another.
  • Shares compound at the US average. Seven percent a year after inflation, everything reinvested. By 2055 the world's shares would be worth more than all the wealth on Earth is today, measured against output. By 2126 they would be worth 61 years of world output.
  • Shares compound at the world average. Since 1900, world shares have returned 5.2% a year after inflation. Even that gentler path makes shares worth 11 years of world output by 2126.

How many Earths?

A share is a claim on future profits. To deliver a 7% return on everything, someone has to produce 7% of the market's value every year, in dividends, buybacks or growing profits. As the market grows faster than the economy, that bill grows faster too.

At the US average, the bill passes the whole world's yearly output by 2088. By 2126 shareholders would need 4.3 times everything the world produces, every year. Today company profits are about a tenth of output. At that share it would take 43 Earths to pay them.

The world average is gentler and still impossible. By 2126 shareholders would need 58% of all output, more than American workers take home today. Their return would outgrow the whole of today's company profits by 2043. At today's profit share it would take 6 Earths.

Why it hasn't happened yet

If the arithmetic is this bad, why does the 7% average exist at all? Because the system as a whole has never compounded it. Over the past century most shareholders spent their dividends, sold shares to retire, and lived through crashes, wars and confiscations. The average return is real for one saver because everyone else, together, kept taking money out.

Part of what looks like return was also a one-off. Shares have become much more expensive relative to the economy, from about half of US output in the 1980s to more than twice it today. That rerating paid investors once. It can't double every few decades forever.

Passive investing takes a result earned by one saver in a system that didn't compound it, and sells it as a plan for everyone, all at once, automatically.

Why that's a bubble

A bubble is a price held up by the money flowing in, not by the cash the asset can produce. Passive investing has three features that fit.

The money comes in regardless of price. Australia's super guarantee rose to 12% of wages in July 2025. Retirement plans in the US and elsewhere enrol workers by default. Every payday, money buys the index at whatever the index costs.

Each dollar moves prices more than a dollar. Economists Xavier Gabaix and Ralph Koijen estimate that $1 flowing into the stock market raises its total value by about $5, because most big holders are bound by rules that stop them selling into a rally. Steady inflows don't just buy shares; they lift the price of all of them.

It buys most of what has already risen most. An index fund holds companies by size, so new money goes mostly to the biggest. Investor Michael Green, the best-known advocate of this argument, calls it a momentum machine. Its other danger is the reverse: when a large generation retires and starts selling to live, the same mechanism works downwards.

What society would have to give up to keep it flowing

The gains can only keep coming if more of the economy is handed to shareholders. Something has to give way.

  • Wages. Workers' share of US output fell to 53.8% in late 2025, the lowest since records began in 1947. Paying shareholders even the world average would eventually need more than that.
  • Competition. The same few index providers own every company in an industry. An owner of every airline has little reason to want a fare war. That pushes towards fat margins and away from lower prices.
  • Policy. When everyone's retirement depends on share prices, governments and central banks are under pressure to keep rates low and rescue markets in every fall. Markets become too big to be allowed to fall.
  • The young. Each generation buys shares from the last at higher prices, and the price of a future retirement keeps rising for people who start saving later.
  • Where capital goes. Money follows size, not merit. New and small companies compete for what is left after the giants are fed.

What happens instead

The projections can't come true, so one or more of these will happen:

  • Returns shrink towards the growth of the economy, slowly disappointing everyone counting on 7%.
  • Prices fall sharply when the flow of new money slows or reverses, as retirement withdrawals overtake contributions.
  • Inflation or taxes quietly reduce what the claims are worth.
  • Politics pushes back, through windfall taxes, wage rules or competition law, to claw back a bigger share for workers.

Each of these keeps the books balanced, and each means savers get less than they were promised.

The strongest case against this

The argument has fair critics, and they deserve a hearing.

  • Prices are still set by active traders. Index funds trade little; active investors and arbitrage traders still decide what companies are worth. A passive majority of fund money is not a passive majority of trading.
  • Investment creates growth. Our model holds world growth at 3% whatever is invested. Reinvested profits build factories and software that raise output, which softens, though doesn't remove, the gap.
  • Companies are not the whole economy. Listed firms can earn abroad, take share from private businesses, and grow profits faster than GDP for a long time.
  • Bubbles can last decades. Being right about arithmetic says little about when prices turn.

None of these changes the core sum. Claims can't compound faster than the economy that pays them, forever. The question is only how the gap closes, and who pays when it does.

The bottom line

For any one saver, an index fund is still a cheap and sensible way to own the market. As a promise to a whole society, it doesn't add up. Compounding at the historical average for a century would need dozens of Earths, or hand nearly everything the world produces to shareholders. Prices built on that promise, and on money that flows in whatever the price, are a bubble. The arithmetic decides that it ends; it doesn't decide when.

How we modelled it

All figures are in today's dollars, projected year by year for 100 years from 2026.

  • Starting values: world listed shares $158 trillion (end of 2025); world output $117 trillion (IMF, 2025); personal wealth $471 trillion (UBS, end of 2024); ETF assets $24.0 trillion (ETFGI, August 2026).
  • World output grows 3% a year after inflation (the IMF puts 2025 at 3.2%). Personal wealth is compared as a multiple of output (about 4× today).
  • Shares compound at 5.2% (world, 1900–2024) or 7% (US, since 1926, about 7.2%) a year after inflation, with every dividend reinvested and nothing withdrawn. That is the promise being tested, not a forecast.
  • Index funds grow at the ETF industry's 2015–2025 rate: from $2.99 trillion to $19.85 trillion, 20.8% a year, or 17.9% after 2.5% inflation. The 2015 figure also counts other exchange-traded products, which makes this growth rate a little low. ETF totals include a small share of actively managed funds.
  • Shareholder bill: the average return times the value of the world's shares, compared with world output. "Earths" divides that bill by company profits at about a tenth of output, roughly where US after-tax corporate profits sit.

The model is deliberately simple: it ignores the feedback from investment to growth, changes in the profit share, and crashes. It shows what the average return implies, not what will happen. Not financial advice.
